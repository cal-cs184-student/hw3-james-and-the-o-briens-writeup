Overview

In this homework, I built up a path tracer piece by piece: first camera rays and intersections, then a BVH to make those intersections fast, then direct and indirect lighting, and finally adaptive sampling. The most satisfying part was seeing how each feature changed the renders. A normal-shaded Cornell box is useful for debugging, but once indirect light and enough samples are in place, it starts to look like an actual scene instead of a collection of triangles.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation and primitive intersection

For each pixel, I cast several slightly different rays, average their colors, and save the result. The small random offsets keep the image from looking blocky. Rays start at the camera, pass through the image plane, and only consider objects inside the clipping range.

I test each ray against spheres and triangles and keep the closest hit. Spheres use a standard ray–sphere test, while triangles use the Möller–Trumbore method. I also blend triangle normals so curved models look smooth instead of faceted.

Normal shading results

For normal shading, I turn the surface direction into a color. The floor is green, surfaces facing the camera lean blue or lavender, and rays that miss everything are black.

All of the images below were rendered with my Part 1 code (normal shading) at 480x360 with the default 1 sample per pixel, and saved with the -f flag rather than taken as screenshots. For example: ./pathtracer -t 8 -r 480 360 -f CBspheres_lambertian.png ../dae/sky/CBspheres_lambertian.dae.

Flat Cornell-box walls each have one color, while spheres and smooth meshes fade between colors. The low-polygon gems still show their faceted shape.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH construction and splitting heuristic

I use a BVH, which is a tree of boxes around groups of objects. If a ray misses a box, it can skip everything inside it. I split objects by the spread of their centers and use a middle split as a fallback when needed.

When tracing a ray, I only visit boxes it touches. At the end, I test the small group of objects in a leaf and keep the nearest hit. This produces the same image while making large scenes much faster.

Large scenes with normal shading

These scenes were rendered with normal shading and the BVH at 480x360, with the default 1 sample per pixel and 8 threads, and saved with the -f flag. The captions list the triangle count along with the BVH build time and render time.

With the BVH, all of these scenes render in roughly a hundredth of a second. Without it, every ray has to check every primitive. Maxplanck already takes 79 seconds single-threaded without acceleration, so larger scenes like CBlucy and wall-e quickly become impractical.

The timing table makes the value of the BVH pretty obvious. Without it, tests per ray exactly match the triangle count, so the work grows linearly with scene size. With it, I only need about 2.5–8.2 primitive tests per ray and render times stay around 0.01–0.06 seconds even for the larger scenes. The speedup grows from roughly 26x on the teapot to over 1,000x on CBbunny and maxplanck. CBbunny actually has the fewest tests per ray, probably because most camera rays hit the large Cornell-box walls and never need to enter the bunny's part of the tree. BVH construction is tiny compared with what it saves; the exact millisecond timings vary a little run to run, but the overall difference is not subtle.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

My two direct lighting implementations

For direct lighting, I calculate how much light reaches a surface and how the material reflects it. I implemented two methods: sampling random directions around the surface, and sampling directions that point toward the lights. In both methods, a shadow ray checks whether something blocks the light.

Light sampling is more efficient because it aims samples where light is likely to be. Random hemisphere sampling spends many samples on dark directions, especially when the light is small.

Uniform hemisphere sampling vs. light sampling: renders

I rendered these at 480x360 (480x480 for the dragon) with 64 pixel samples, 32 light samples, and 8 threads. Since these came from my final renderer rather than the Part 3 checkpoint, I also used:

  • -m 1, which makes the renderer return exactly zero-bounce plus one-bounce (direct) lighting. That is the Part 3 result, with no indirect bounces.
  • -a 64 0.05, which turns off Part 5's adaptive sampling here: with at most 64 samples, the convergence check never runs, so every pixel gets the full sample count.

The spec uses -m 6 in Part 3 only because indirect lighting did not exist yet at that point.

Light sampling is noticeably cleaner in the bunny and sphere scenes, and it also works for the dragon's directional and ambient lights. Hemisphere sampling misses those lights entirely.

Soft shadow noise vs. number of light rays

Here I focus on CBbunny.dae, which has one area light, rendered with light sampling at 480x360 with 1 sample per pixel and 1, 4, 16, and 64 light rays (-s 1 -l {1, 4, 16, 64} -m 1).

With more light rays, the bunny's soft shadow becomes smoother and the speckling drops sharply.

In a soft shadow, each pixel estimates how much of the light it can see. More light samples make that estimate smoother, but take longer to render.

Comparing uniform hemisphere sampling and light sampling

To compare the two methods at an equal, low budget, I also rendered CBbunny with 1 sample per pixel and 16 light samples (-s 1 -l 16 -m 1) using each method:

At the same sample budget, light sampling is much less noisy. The hemisphere render in the 1 spp × 16 comparison is mostly dark with a few bright dots, while the light-sampled version is already close to clean. Both estimators are unbiased; the difference is variance. A random hemisphere direction rarely lands on the small area light, so most samples contribute nothing. Light sampling aims every sample toward a light, so it only gets zeroed out when the light is blocked or behind the surface. It also handles delta lights, which explains the black dragon in the hemisphere render. In my tests it was faster for the bunny and dragon and about even for the spheres. The ceiling glow around the light in the hemisphere images is from the area light's directionality, not noise.

Part 4: Global Illumination

My indirect lighting implementation

Global illumination lets light bounce between surfaces instead of stopping after the first hit. I sample a new direction from each diffuse surface, trace another ray, and add that bounced light to the pixel. This creates soft shadows, indirect light, and color bleeding.

I limit the number of bounces with -m. Russian roulette randomly ends some long paths while keeping the final image correct on average, which makes deeper renders much faster.

Global illumination renders

Render settings for every image in Part 4: 480x360 (the dragon is 480x480), 8 threads, saved with -f. Every image uses 4 light rays (-l 4) and 1024 samples per pixel unless the caption says otherwise. I passed -a 1024 0.05 only to keep Part 5's adaptive sampling from stopping pixels early: with at most 1024 samples, its convergence check never runs, so every pixel gets the full sample count.

These three scenes were rendered with full global illumination (direct and indirect) at -m 5.

Compared with the direct-only renders, the ceiling and upper corners are no longer black and the shadows are much softer. The other clear change is color bleeding: nearby surfaces pick up red from the red wall and blue from the blue wall.

Only direct vs. only indirect illumination

For this comparison I used CBbunny.dae at 1024 samples per pixel and 4 light rays. The direct-only image uses -m 1, which gives zero-bounce plus one-bounce lighting. For the indirect-only image, I followed the spec and temporarily edited the code in a separate build:

  • In at_least_one_bounce_radiance, I skip the one_bounce_radiance term at the first hit (r.depth == max_ray_depth) but keep it at every later hit along the path.
  • In est_radiance_global_illumination, I drop the zero-bounce term.

That way the image (rendered with -m 5) only contains light that bounced at least twice, i.e. bounces 2 through 5. My submitted code is unchanged.

Direct light gives the scene its bright surfaces and sharper shadows. Indirect light fills in the ceiling and shadows, softens the image, and creates the red and blue color bleeding.

Rendering only the m-th bounce of light

These are CBbunny.dae renders with isAccumBounces set to false (-o 0), so each image shows only the light from the m-th bounce, for max_ray_depth 0 through 5.

The first bounce gives direct lighting. Later bounces are dimmer, but they light the ceiling, soften shadows, and add color bleeding. This is the extra realism a simple direct-light renderer misses.

Compared with rasterization: a typical rasterizer computes only direct lighting from the light sources (like m = 1), so the ceiling would be black, the shadows would be pitch black, and there would be no color bleeding. The 2nd and 3rd bounces supply exactly that missing light: they light up the ceiling, fill the shadows with soft light, and carry the wall colors onto nearby surfaces. That indirect light is a big part of why the path-traced image looks physically real.

Varying the samples per pixel

For this comparison I rendered CBspheres_lambertian.dae with 4 light rays and -m 5 at 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 64, and 1024 samples per pixel.

At one sample per pixel the image is very noisy. It steadily becomes smoother as the sample count increases, and 1024 samples is nearly clean.

More samples make the image smoother, but also increase render time. That tradeoff is why adaptive sampling is useful.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

What adaptive sampling is and how I implemented it

Adaptive sampling gives hard-to-render pixels more work and stops early on simple ones. Every 64 samples, I check whether a pixel's color has settled down enough. If it has, I stop; otherwise, I keep sampling until it reaches the maximum.

The rate image shows where samples went: red pixels needed many samples, blue pixels finished early, and green pixels are in between. Noisy shadows and indirectly lit areas usually need more samples than bright, directly lit walls.

Adaptive sampling results

I rendered both scenes with the same settings:

./pathtracer -t 8 -s 2048 -a 64 0.05 -l 1 -m 5 -r 480 360 -f <name>.png <scene>

So each pixel can use up to 2048 samples, gets checked every 64 samples, and uses a 5% tolerance. The renderer also writes the matching _rate.png image automatically.

Rendered image Sampling rate image

Both final renders look clean. The rate images also make it clear where the extra work went:

Red areas—especially ceilings, soft shadows, and darker surfaces—need the most samples. Blue areas, such as the light, background, and directly lit walls, settle quickly.

The light and background settle quickly because their samples barely change. Indirectly lit ceilings and soft shadows vary more, so I spend more samples there.
